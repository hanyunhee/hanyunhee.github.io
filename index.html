As an undergraduate I co-founded Shelfie, a reading-log platform, and then interned at a startup to learn how startups are supposed to work. What struck me was how hard startups, mine included, work to look like startups. Titles gave way to nicknames overnight, and companies down the hall rebranded themselves as AI startups. I study what this ideal of the startup is, why new ventures work so hard to live up to it, and what the practices it inspires actually do for them.

My current work examines:

  • how reducing hierarchy can push people apart rather than bring them together, based on 84 in-depth interviews (single-authored);
  • how support intermediaries shape which outsider ideas come to be seen as novel (with Prof. Eunki Ro and Prof. Sang-Joon Kim, under review at Research Policy);
  • how startups that did nothing wrong lose funding when a large firm in their category faces a scandal, using regression analysis in Stata on data covering 51,738 U.S. startups (single-authored);
  • how a social enterprise keeps its core model, triangular mentoring, while changing its form to work with large corporate partners and expand globally (ongoing field study with Prof. Boram Do, Prof. Sang-Joon Kim, and Prof. Sung-Chul Noh).

The study on scandal spillover was named a finalist (1 of 6 shortlisted papers) in the Graduate Student Paper Competition at the 2026 KASBA Fall Conference, with final results to be announced in November. The study on hierarchy was presented in two Professional Development Workshops at the 2026 Academy of Management Annual Meeting and at the 2026 JMS Publishing Workshop.

A flat organization

Startups prize flatness. Job titles give way to nicknames in the hope of freer communication and more new ideas. But does looking flat make an organization flat, and does it deliver what flattening promised?

Working Paper

Dehierarchized but Not Connected: The Decoupling of Vertical and Lateral Governance

Han, Y.

Drawing on 84 in-depth interviews with university students analyzed with the Gioia method, this paper shows that reducing hierarchy narrows vertical distance but also weakens lateral ties. Clear senior–junior roles had given people a legitimate script for asking peers for help; without them, people knew each other less and held back.

A self-made innovator

Startups present themselves as original pioneers pushing their own ideas forward. Yet much of their newness is shaped by others: by intermediaries that decide which ideas count as novel, and by the categories startups join to borrow legitimacy.

Under Review

Made at the Gate: How Support Intermediaries Constitute Outsider Innovation in Social Entrepreneurship

Ro, E., Han, Y., & Kim, S.-J.

This qualitative study shows that support intermediaries filter and translate outsider ideas, shaping which of them come to be recognized as novel, rather than novelty arising from the ventures alone. Under review at Research Policy.

Working Paper

When Big Firms Stumble, Startups Pay: Category Spillover and Startup Responses

Han, Y., & Kim, S.-J.

Using Facebook's 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal and Crunchbase data on 51,738 U.S. startups founded in 2015–2021, with regression analysis in Stata, this paper shows that social-platform startups with no part in the scandal became less likely to raise funding. The penalty was largest in wellness and health, closest to the scandal's privacy concerns, and among startups evaluated after the event.

Beneath the image

If the ideal startup is partly an image, what lies beneath it? Some ventures keep changing their form to fit what partners and funders expect, while holding on to the core that makes them who they are.

Where I am goingAcross these studies, what ventures show the world often differs from what keeps them alive. I want to ask what the ideal of a “startup” is that ventures conform to, how it is built, and when conforming to it helps a venture and when it costs the venture its core. I plan to trace this ideal in the language of founders, investors, and intermediaries, and then test its consequences with large-scale venture data.

Where I am goingMy studies point to a gap between the startup that ventures try to look like and the one they are. Two questions remain open. What is the ideal of the startup, and how is it built and spread? And when does living up to it help a venture, and when does it hurt? I plan to trace this ideal in the language of founders, investors, and intermediaries, and then test its consequences with large-scale venture data.
