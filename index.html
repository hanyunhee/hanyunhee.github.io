After interning at a startup and co-founding Shelfie, a reading-log platform, I turned to research to understand why some startups survive and others do not. Few things are harder to start, or harder to keep alive. With little money and little time, a small choice today can decide whether a company is still standing tomorrow, and many startups end up trading away their core values just to survive. I find that tension fascinating. I study how new ventures hold on to what makes them their own while outside forces push them to change.

My current work examines:

  • how reducing hierarchy can push people apart rather than bring them together, based on 84 in-depth interviews (single-authored);
  • how startups that did nothing wrong lose funding when a large firm in their category faces a scandal, using data on 51,738 U.S. startups (single-authored);
  • how support intermediaries shape which outsider ideas come to be seen as novel (with Eunki Ro and Sang-Joon Kim, submitted to Research Policy);
  • how a social enterprise keeps its core model, triangular mentoring, while changing its form to work with large corporate partners and expand globally (ongoing field study with Boram Do, Sang-Joon Kim, and Sung-Chul Noh).

The first study was presented in two Professional Development Workshops at the 2026 Academy of Management Annual Meeting and at the 2026 JMS Publishing Workshop. The second was selected as a finalist, one of six papers, in the Graduate Student Paper Competition at the 2026 KASBA Fall Conference, with final results announced in November.

Research

How do new ventures hold on to what makes them their own while outside forces push them to change?

I follow this question in three moves: what ventures build on the inside, what pushes on them from the outside, and what they choose to keep.

  1. 01Inside

    Startups build their own structures, and many flatten hierarchy in the hope of more open, supportive relationships. Studying university students, the community where many founding teams form, I found the opposite. Reducing hierarchy narrowed the distance between seniors and juniors but also weakened ties among peers, because clear roles had given people a legitimate way to ask for help.

    Dehierarchized but Not Connected · 84 interviews
  2. 02Outside

    But ventures do not move on their own. Support intermediaries filter and translate which outsider ideas come to count as novel. Shocks also spread across a category: after Cambridge Analytica, social-platform startups that did nothing wrong became less likely to raise funding, most sharply in wellness and health.

    Made at the Gate · When Big Firms Stumble, Startups Pay · 51,738 startups
  3. 03Back to the core

    Are startups then powerless? Not entirely. Within the same stigmatized category, startups designed to let users control their own exposure were more likely to raise funding. And JUMP, a social enterprise working with large corporations, has kept its triangular mentoring model by changing its form rather than giving it up.

    When Big Firms Stumble, Startups Pay · JUMP field study

Working Papers

Working Paper

Dehierarchized but Not Connected: The Decoupling of Vertical and Lateral Governance

Single-authored

Organizations flatten hierarchy expecting more open and collaborative relationships. Drawing on 84 in-depth interviews with university students analyzed with the Gioia method, this paper shows that reducing hierarchy narrows vertical distance but also weakens lateral ties. Clear senior–junior roles had given people a legitimate script for asking peers for help; without them, people knew each other less and held back.

Working Paper

When Big Firms Stumble, Startups Pay: Category Spillover and Startup Responses

Single-authored

Using Facebook's 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal and Crunchbase data on 51,738 U.S. startups founded in 2015–2021, this paper shows that social-platform startups with no part in the scandal became less likely to raise funding. The penalty was largest in wellness and health, the categories closest to the scandal's privacy concerns, and among startups evaluated after the event. Startups designed to let users control their own exposure were more likely to raise funding despite the category stigma.

Where does the novelty of outsider ventures come from? This qualitative study shows that support intermediaries filter and translate outsider ideas, shaping which of them come to be recognized as novel, rather than novelty arising from the ventures alone.
