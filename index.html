After interning at a startup and co-founding Shelfie, a reading-log platform, I turned to research to understand why some startups survive and others do not. Few things are harder to start, or harder to keep alive. With little money and little time, a small choice today can decide whether a company is still standing tomorrow, and many startups end up trading away their core values just to survive. I find that tension fascinating. I study how new ventures hold on to what makes them their own while outside forces push them to change.

My current work examines:

  • how support intermediaries shape which outsider ideas come to be seen as novel (with Prof. Eunki Ro and Prof. Sang-Joon Kim, under review at Research Policy);
  • how startups that did nothing wrong lose funding when a large firm in their category faces a scandal, using regression analysis in Stata on data covering 51,738 U.S. startups (single-authored);
  • how reducing hierarchy can push people apart rather than bring them together, based on 84 in-depth interviews (single-authored);
  • how a social enterprise keeps its core model, triangular mentoring, while changing its form to work with large corporate partners and expand globally (ongoing field study with Prof. Boram Do, Prof. Sang-Joon Kim, and Prof. Sung-Chul Noh).

The study on scandal spillover was named a finalist (1 of 6 shortlisted papers) in the Graduate Student Paper Competition at the 2026 KASBA Fall Conference, with final results to be announced in November. The study on hierarchy was presented in two Professional Development Workshops at the 2026 Academy of Management Annual Meeting and at the 2026 JMS Publishing Workshop.

I am applying to PhD programs in Management for Fall 2027.

01 Inside

Startups reshape themselves to grow, flattening hierarchies and inventing new ways of working in the belief that the right structure will carry them forward. But do the structures they choose for themselves actually make them stronger?

Working Paper

Dehierarchized but Not Connected: The Decoupling of Vertical and Lateral Governance

Han, Y.

Drawing on 84 in-depth interviews with university students analyzed with the Gioia method, this paper shows that reducing hierarchy narrows vertical distance but also weakens lateral ties. Clear senior–junior roles had given people a legitimate script for asking peers for help; without them, people knew each other less and held back.

02 Outside

Look closer, and much of that newness is borrowed, shaped to look legitimate in the eyes of others. And whatever is built to please the outside is ruled by it too, so startups sway with every gust, from fleeting trends to shocks they never saw coming.

Working Paper

When Big Firms Stumble, Startups Pay: Category Spillover and Startup Responses

Han, Y., & Kim, S.-J.

Using Facebook's 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal and Crunchbase data on 51,738 U.S. startups founded in 2015–2021, with regression analysis in Stata, this paper shows that social-platform startups with no part in the scandal became less likely to raise funding. The penalty was largest in wellness and health, closest to the scandal's privacy concerns, and among startups evaluated after the event.
